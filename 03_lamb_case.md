# The Lamb Counterfactual: A Test of C∞

## Setup
Compare two hypothetical situations with matched starting conditions: (A) a predator kills a young lamb before reproduction; (B) the predator obtains equally safe, adequate nutrition from a naturally deceased animal while the lamb survives. The alternative is a thought experiment; natural carcasses may be scarce, unsafe, or important to scavengers.

## Pathways to investigate
- **Predator:** calories, disease exposure, foraging effort, survival, and future hunting.
- **Lamb:** survival, reproduction probability, offspring survival, grazing, waste, and eventual death.
- **Vegetation:** selective grazing, trampling, seed dispersal, soil compaction, and plant competition.
- **Microbes and scavengers:** living microbiomes, carcass decomposition, nutrient redistribution, pathogen dynamics, and competing consumers.
- **Population and habitat:** density dependence, competition, predator-prey stability, carrying capacity, and climate variation.
- **Foregone futures:** descendants and interactions that do not occur under A; ecological changes that do not occur under B.

## Critical caveats
None of the directions above is guaranteed. Grazing can increase or decrease plant growth. A surviving lamb may or may not reproduce. Predation can stabilize populations. A naturally deceased animal may already be claimed by other organisms. Microbes are transformed and redistributed, not simply erased. More descendants or more meat is not automatically greater ecosystem efficiency.

## Test
Specify a real ecosystem, measured parameters, and matched counterfactuals. Model outcomes at multiple horizons with uncertainty intervals. Compare predictions with established predator-prey and food-web models. Report both effects and errors, including surprises.

## Question
Does this framing reveal consequential pathways or predictive relationships that standard ecological models miss?
